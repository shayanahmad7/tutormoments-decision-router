AI tutors must help students without completing the mathematical reasoning they need to practice. Using TutorMoments-Preview, a dataset of real tutoring conversations, we examine this problem in two ways. First, we code the work left for learners in 38 moments. Explicit instructions about balancing support and challenge produced more replies inviting connections, explanations, or strategies than general tutoring guidance. Second, we test whether a separate model can improve a tutor by choosing scaffolding or rigor before the tutor replies. We compare Jev and OpenAI Decisions with a tutor choosing its own approach and with fixed instructions across 520 moments. Automated first-reply pass rates were 68.7\% for self-choice, 72.9\% with Jev, 73.5\% with Decisions, and 74.0\% with rigor instructions at every moment. The results show modest gains over self-choice but no advantage over the strongest simple control, highlighting the distinction between selecting a teaching direction and delivering appropriate support.
