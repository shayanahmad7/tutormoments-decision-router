Jev's decisions matched the assigned category in 376/520 moments (72.3\%); OpenAI Decisions matched it in 386 (74.2\%). For reply quality, Jev-guided replies passed in 379 cases and Decisions-guided replies in 382, compared with 357 for self-choice (Table~\ref{tab:routing}). These were gains of 4.2 percentage points for Jev (95\% confidence interval: $-1.0$ to $9.2$) and 4.8 points for Decisions ($0.2$ to $9.5$). Always using rigor guidance passed 385 cases, slightly more than either decision system. Jev improved rigor passes from 149 to 180 while reducing scaffolding passes from 208 to 199.

The evaluator also mattered: on the same 32 cases, Gemini passed 20 self-choice and 19 Jev replies; Claude passed 16 and 22. They agreed on 75/96 reply judgments (78.1\%).

Correct category selection did not guarantee a good response: 56 of Jev's 376 category matches still led to failed replies, while 59 of its 144 mismatches passed. In one case, both systems correctly selected scaffolding for a learner working on $1{,}000-10$, but Qwen's reply returned to an earlier $338+586$ problem.

There was no estimated speed advantage. Qwen's median time to begin answering was 1.20 seconds for self-choice. Adding separately measured decision durations gave estimates of 1.43 seconds with Jev and 1.60 with Decisions. These are reconstructed estimates, not measured live-pipeline times. Estimated model costs per 1,000 replies were \$0.59, \$0.90, and \$1.25, respectively, excluding evaluation.
