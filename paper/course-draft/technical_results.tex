Jev-guided replies passed in 379/520 cases and Decisions-guided replies in 382, compared with 357 for self-choice (Table~\ref{tab:routing}). These were gains of 4.2 percentage points for Jev (95\% student-resampling interval: $-1.0$ to $9.2$) and 4.8 points for Decisions ($0.2$ to $9.5$). However, always using rigor guidance passed 385 cases, slightly more than either decision system. Jev improved performance on rigor opportunities from 149 to 180 passes, while reducing scaffolding passes from 208 to 199.

In the stricter 346-moment mathematics set, Jev's advantage over self-choice was only 3 cases, or 0.9 points (interval $-5.3$ to $6.5$). Evaluation was also sensitive to the judge. On the same 32 cases, Gemini passed 20 self-choice and 19 Jev replies; Claude passed 16 and 22, respectively. The two evaluators agreed on 75 of the 96 individual reply judgments (78.1\%).

Correct category selection did not guarantee a good response. Of Jev's 376 category matches, 320 selected replies passed; 59 of its 144 mismatches also passed. In one case, both decision systems correctly selected scaffolding for a learner working on $1{,}000-10$, but Qwen's reply returned to an earlier $338+586$ problem. Even selecting by the dataset label passed only 438/520 cases. The remaining failures concern how the tutor carries out its instruction, not just which instruction is chosen.

We found no speed advantage. Qwen's median time to begin answering was 1.20 seconds for self-choice. Adding the separately measured decision durations gave estimated totals of 1.43 seconds with Jev and 1.60 with Decisions. These are reconstructed estimates, because saved decisions selected pre-generated replies; we did not time a live end-to-end system. Estimated model costs per 1,000 replies were \$0.59, \$0.90, and \$1.25, respectively, excluding evaluation.
