\subsection{Interpretation of qualitative findings}
We began with qualitative coding to understand what changes inside a tutor's response when its instructions change. The evaluation-aware prompt left explanation, connection, or strategy work in 16 of 38 replies, compared with two under the plain prompt, while complete-answer giving fell from 21 to five. This suggests that explicit pedagogical guidance can shift the same model from completing the task toward eliciting a mathematical contribution. Yet 20 evaluation-aware replies still left only calculation or cued recognition, and just two left strategy or argument with meaningful discretion. The improvement was mainly toward explaining within a tutor-defined path.

Our two codes make that distinction visible. In E01, asking a question left only a missing-factor calculation; in E06, a supplied procedure still left the denominator explanation open. E11 shows why answer-giving alone is an incomplete measure: the tutor supplied $17\%$ but left the learner to explain why $170\%$ was wrong. For educators and designers, the useful question is what thinking the response still requires. A prompt can request dialogue while supplying nearly all the reasoning. The human reference also frequently left little new work, but these selected moments and first-response codes do not rank human teaching overall. TutorMoments deliberately includes missed opportunities in human tutoring \citep{zhang2026tutormoments}.

\subsection{Utility of decision-guided tutoring}
The technical study asked whether a fast decision system could make a smaller tutor more effective without relying on a larger model for every reply. The original benchmark gives a reason to pursue this: evaluation-aware Claude Opus 4.8 achieved 85.8\% appropriate scaffolding and 83.1\% appropriate rigor, but the authors reported that it and Gemini 2.5 Pro averaged over ten seconds to complete a response \citep{zhang2026tutormoments}. The current project website reports newer models combining strong scores with lower latency and treats cost as a separate concern \citep{ai22026tutormomentswebsite}. Affordable, responsive tutoring therefore remains a design objective, rather than a reason to assume all strong models are slow. Our experiment tested decision-guided instructions as one route toward that objective.

The added decision step did not achieve the intended advantage in this experiment. Jev and Decisions improved the pass rate over Qwen choosing its own approach, but both fell below the simpler condition that always supplied rigor instructions. They also added estimated cost and delay. For this configuration, the results favor improving direct instructions before adding a separate chooser. They do not show that every standalone language model is better than every decision model. The original leaderboard establishes that strong tutors exist, but its multi-turn scores cannot be ranked directly against our first-reply scores.

The failures help explain what needs improvement. Matching the assigned category still produced 56 failed Jev-guided replies, including one that answered an earlier problem. Selecting \emph{scaffolding} does not identify the exact misconception or ensure that the tutor follows the current task. Conversely, fixed rigor guidance could still produce support: 202 of its 520 replies combined scaffolding and rigor. This helps explain why it scored well without a separate choice at each moment. The binary decision may be too coarse to add much beyond instructions that already permit both behaviors.

\subsection{Implications and future research}
Together, the studies provide a way to examine both the response and the system producing it. The codebook asks what mathematical work remains; the experiment asks whether an added component improves that work's delivery. TutorMoments supplies shared conversation histories and teacher-informed targets, so future designs can be compared on the same situations. A next experiment could select a specific action, such as requesting a justification, giving a representation, or leaving an independent step, and use the coding framework to check what reasoning survives in the reply. Smaller models could also be trained on these distinctions. Such variants should be compared with strong direct prompts and timed as complete pipelines on previously unused students or moments.

Our contribution at this stage is the coding framework and a reproducible test showing that this decision-guided design has not yet earned its added complexity. Prompt development used these data, automated evaluators disagreed on some replies, and student learning was not measured. These limits identify the next tests needed before classroom claims. AI tools assisted initial coding, programming, evaluation, and drafting; the team reviewed and agreed on the final qualitative codes.
