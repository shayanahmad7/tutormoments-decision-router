# Draft technical methods text

We examined whether separating instructional choice from response generation improved a smaller tutor beyond detailed prompting alone. We used all 520 released TutorMoments moments, comprising 260 scaffolding and 260 rigor conditions from 89 students. Every generator received the complete released conversation prefix and student metadata; assigned categories, teacher comments, personas and future continuation were excluded. The corpus had already been inspected during prompt development, so this experiment is an internal exploratory evaluation. We also report the pre-existing 346-moment mathematics frame used in the qualitative study, with exact source-ID matching.

Qwen3-32B generated three next replies at each moment: detailed self-choice, forced scaffolding and forced rigor. In self-choice, the tutor received both instructional policies and chose how to respond; the forced conditions supplied one of the same policies. These prompts were frozen before generation. Temperature was zero, the output cap was 384 tokens, and the model used non-thinking mode through a pinned DeepInfra endpoint on OpenRouter. We generated 1,560 distinct replies. Archived Jev prompt-C decisions and OpenAI Decisions outputs selected between the two forced-policy replies. Fixed-policy, assigned-category and opposite-category controls selected from those same outputs, yielding paired counterfactual policy comparisons rather than independent additional generations. The archived routers had matched 376/520 and 386/520 assigned categories, respectively, and were selected from prior exploration rather than an unseen test set.

Gemini 2.5 Flash rated the three replies in a seeded random order under anonymous candidate identifiers. The evaluator received the conversation but not the assigned category, routing output, condition names or teacher comments. It coded scaffolding and rigor as separate observable behaviors, permitting both, alongside over-helping, definite mathematical error, context fit and uncertainty. A passing reply had to exhibit the assigned direction, avoid over-helping and detected mathematical error, fit the current context, and be complete and valid. We separately retained the simpler direction-plus-no-over-help outcome. A prespecified 32-case, student-distinct, category-balanced Claude Sonnet 4.6 audit assessed evaluator sensitivity. These are automated first-reply ratings, not the publisher's multi-turn leaderboard score or observed student learning.

The primary contrast is Jev-directed policy versus detailed self-choice. We report both fixed policies and Decisions-directed policy to test whether any gain requires adaptive routing. Differences use paired moments and 10,000 student-cluster bootstrap resamples. A branch-headroom analysis identifies cases where both policy replies passed, one passed or neither passed, while error-propagation tables distinguish reference-label agreement from subsequent reply success. Timings record generation time to first visible answer during concurrent requests. Adding archived router latency produces an explicitly reconstructed serial estimate, not a measured deployment speedup. Evaluation costs are separate from estimated costs for the selected reply and router.

# Short limitations paragraph

The preview was used in earlier prompt exploration, the decision outputs were archived, and each policy produced one sampled reply per moment. Consequently, the study assesses internal consistency and policy effects, not unseen-data generalization or deployment performance. Automated evaluators may disagree with expert teaching judgments; source categories describe a desired instructional direction rather than forbidding combined support and challenge. The paired outputs and repeated moments per student do not constitute independent replications, and no learner outcomes or human validation are inferred.
